how motors work|An electric motor runs current through a coil inside a magnetic field; the field pushes the coil (Lorentz force), and a commutator or electronic switching keeps reversing current at the right moment so the push keeps the shaft spinning instead of settling in place.
torque|Torque is a rotational force: torque = force x distance from the pivot (lever arm). More torque means more twisting power at the axle, which is why low gears give more torque but less top speed than high gears.
why does ice float|Water expands when it freezes because its molecules lock into a hexagonal crystal lattice with more open space than liquid water, making ice about 9% less dense - so it floats instead of sinking like most frozen liquids.
surface tension|Surface tension comes from water molecules pulling on each other (hydrogen bonds) more strongly than on the air above, making the surface act like a stretched elastic skin - strong enough to support a water strider or a floating needle.
how solar panels work|Sunlight hitting a silicon photovoltaic cell knocks electrons loose; the cell's internal structure (a p-n junction) forces those freed electrons to flow in one direction, producing DC current that an inverter converts to usable AC.
fuse|A fuse is a thin wire or strip designed to melt and break the circuit if current exceeds its rating, protecting wiring and devices from overheating or fire. Unlike a circuit breaker, a blown fuse must be replaced, not just reset.
how a microwave works|A microwave oven generates 2.45 GHz radio waves with a magnetron; that frequency makes water molecules in food flip back and forth rapidly, and that friction between molecules generates heat throughout the food, not just on the surface.
refraction|Refraction is light bending when it passes between materials of different density (e.g. air to water) because light changes speed in each material. It's why a straw looks bent in a glass of water and why lenses can focus light.
why is the sky red at sunset|At sunset, sunlight passes through far more atmosphere than at noon, so most of the blue light scatters away before reaching your eyes (Rayleigh scattering), leaving the longer red and orange wavelengths to dominate.
palindrome|A palindrome reads the same forwards and backwards, ignoring spaces/punctuation/case: "racecar", "madam", "a man a plan a canal panama". Checking one just compares the string to its reverse.
queue,queue data structure|A queue is first-in, first-out (FIFO): items are added at the back (enqueue) and removed from the front (dequeue), like a line at a checkout. Used for task scheduling, print jobs and breadth-first search.
stack data structure|A stack is last-in, first-out (LIFO): the last item pushed on is the first one popped off, like a stack of plates. Used for function call tracking (the call stack), undo history and depth-first search.
linked list|A linked list is a chain of nodes, each holding a value and a pointer to the next node, so inserting or removing in the middle is O(1) once you're there (unlike an array, which must shift elements). Lookup by index is slower than an array, O(n).
compiler vs interpreter|A compiler translates the whole program into machine code ahead of time, producing a standalone executable (C, Rust). An interpreter reads and runs the source line by line at execution time (classic Python, Bash). Many languages (modern Python, Java) blend both with bytecode and a virtual machine.
firewall|A firewall filters network traffic by rules (allowed/blocked ports, IPs, protocols), sitting between a trusted network and an untrusted one. A basic firewall just checks packet headers; a stateful one tracks whole connections for smarter filtering.
proxy,network proxy|A proxy sits between you and the internet, forwarding your requests and returning the responses - used to hide your IP, cache content, filter traffic, or bypass geographic or network restrictions. A VPN is a proxy that also encrypts the whole connection.
packet sniffer|A packet sniffer (e.g. Wireshark) captures network traffic passing through an interface so you can inspect it. On a switched network you normally only see your own traffic unless you're on a hub, use ARP spoofing, or enable port mirroring.
wardriving|Wardriving is driving or walking around with a receiver logging nearby wireless networks (Wi-Fi SSIDs, sometimes Bluetooth or sub-GHz signals) and their locations, originally used to map open or weakly secured access points.
brute force pin attack|A PIN brute force tries every combination in order (0000-9999 for a 4-digit PIN is only 10,000 tries). Lockout after a few wrong attempts, increasing delays, and wipe-after-N-tries policies are what actually stop this in practice, not PIN length alone.
car alarm,how car alarms work|Most car alarms watch sensors (door/hood/trunk switches, a shock/tilt sensor, sometimes glass-break microphones) and sound a siren plus flash lights if one trips without the key fob disarming it first via a coded radio signal.
keyless entry,remote keyless|Keyless entry fobs send a coded radio signal (often rolling code, 300-900 MHz) to unlock/start the car. Passive keyless (walk-up-and-go) constantly exchanges a short-range low-frequency challenge/response with the fob, which relay attacks exploit by extending that range.
can bus|CAN bus (Controller Area Network) is how a modern vehicle's computers (engine, brakes, dashboard, etc.) talk to each other over two twisted wires, broadcasting short messages that any connected module can read - fast and reliable but with no built-in authentication by default.
obd2|OBD-II is the standard diagnostic port (usually under the dashboard) that exposes a car's CAN bus for reading engine codes, sensor data and performance info with a scan tool - the same port security researchers use to probe vehicle networks.
smart lock|Smart locks replace or add to a mechanical lock with an electronic actuator controlled over Bluetooth, Wi-Fi or Zigbee, often via a phone app or keypad. Security depends entirely on the app/firmware and pairing process - a physically strong deadbolt can still have a weak digital front door.
skimmer,card skimmer|A skimmer is a hidden device fitted over or inside a card reader (ATM, gas pump) that copies a card's magnetic stripe data, sometimes paired with a hidden camera or fake keypad to also capture the PIN. Chip and tap payments are far harder to skim than a swipe.
dictionary attack|A dictionary attack tries real words and known common/leaked passwords instead of every possible combination, which is why "password123" falls in seconds while a random 16-character passphrase does not - it isn't in anyone's dictionary.
steganography|Steganography hides data inside other data so its presence isn't obvious - e.g. tweaking the least significant bits of an image's pixels to embed a message. Unlike encryption, which scrambles data openly, steganography tries to hide that there is a secret at all.
checksum error|A checksum error means the data's checksum (or CRC) computed on arrival doesn't match the one sent with it, meaning the data was corrupted in transit or storage. The receiver typically discards it and asks for a resend rather than trusting damaged data.
overclocking|Overclocking runs a chip above its rated clock speed for more performance, usually needing more voltage and better cooling to stay stable, and it can shorten the chip's lifespan or cause crashes if pushed past what the silicon and cooling can handle.
impedance|Impedance is AC resistance: it includes plain resistance plus the extra opposition from capacitors and inductors, which varies with frequency (unlike plain resistance). Matching impedance between a source and load (e.g. speaker to amplifier) avoids wasted or reflected signal power.
